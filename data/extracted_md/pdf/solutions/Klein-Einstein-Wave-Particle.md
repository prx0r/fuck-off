             Niels Bohr, who was in a unique position to know,

physicists from many countries,” and “the combination of dif­

particular one of the lines of approach appear to be the principal
way in which quantum physics developed. Understandably, but
nonetheless unfortunately, many of the accounts of the period,
including some of the memoirs written by those who played lead­

picture of the development that one gets from such accounts,
interesting though they may be for their personal, first-hand de­
tails, lacks just that variety and complexity peculiar to the prin­
cipal achievement of twentieth century physics.
   The most common form that the oversimplification takes is an
almost exclusive concentration on the problems'of atomic struc­
ture and atomic spectra from Bohr’s work in 1913 to the new
quantum mechanics of 1925-26. Such a concentration necessarily
implies a neglect of other problems, problems that may have con­
cerned a smaller number of theorists but that figured in a major
