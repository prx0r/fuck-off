Yes. I think this is potentially **the deepest part of the consumer thesis**.

The important conceptual shift is:

> Consumers are not merely the audience for Pāṭala's research.
> **Their attempts to understand the tradition become measurements of the research object itself.**

That is substantially stronger than “use analytics to make better content.”

There is now direct evidence for the first step of this. A June 2026 study mapped 1,340 real questions asked to an AI teaching assistant onto a curriculum prerequisite graph. Question frequency correlated with independently reported topic difficulty, meaning conversation logs acted as a live diagnostic signal for where learners were struggling. ([arXiv][1])

Pāṭala can push that idea far beyond course analytics.

# 1. Questions tell us not only what is difficult, but **what the graph is missing**

This distinction matters.

Imagine 400 users ask:

> Why does recognition require memory?

Easy.

Pāṭala already contains:

```text
recognition
memory
continuity
IPK passages
argument
```

So that's a **coverage/explanation problem**.

But suppose people repeatedly ask:

> Does recognition require the earlier and later self to be numerically identical?

And Pāṭala cannot cleanly map the question onto an existing graph relation.

Now that's different.

```text
USER QUESTION
      ↓
semantic parse
      ↓
NO SATISFACTORY GRAPH PATH
      ↓
ONTOLOGY GAP
```

Conversational-AI research treats precisely this kind of thing as **ontology expansion**: real users inevitably introduce new intents, concepts and relations that a static ontology did not anticipate. ([ACL Anthology][2])

And research on KB question-answering shows that questions can be unanswerable for structurally distinct reasons such as missing facts, missing schema or a knowledge base whose scope simply does not cover what the user is asking. ([ACL Anthology][3])

For Pāṭala that gives us a very useful classifier:

```text
WHY COULDN'T WE ANSWER?

A. evidence missing
B. interpretation unresolved
C. concept missing
D. relation missing
E. scholarship not ingested
F. question genuinely open
G. question malformed/confused
H. answer exists but explainer failed
```

That's an **automatic research backlog generated by consumers**.

# 2. New concepts can literally enter Pāṭala through users

There's existing precedent here.

A Pinterest production system used machine learning plus human verification to place newly emerging concepts into an existing knowledge graph. Their system predicted where a new concept belonged, then humans verified it; the authors report dramatically faster graph expansion than fully manual placement. ([arXiv][4])

Pāṭala could do something very similar.

Suppose people repeatedly use:

> nondual perception

But Pāṭala has no canonical object corresponding precisely to that phrase.

The system observes:

```text
"nondual perception"
      │
      ├── frequently co-occurs with recognition
      ├── linked to perception
      ├── linked to post-recognition transformation
      ├── references Vijñānabhairava
      └── frequently compared with Dzogchen
```

Agent proposes:

```text
CandidateConcept CC-881

label:
nondual perception

possible parents:
  perception
  recognition
  liberation

possibly maps to:
  X
  Y

status:
USER_EMERGENT_CONCEPT
```

Expert/reviewer decides:

* genuine traditional category;
* modern analytic convenience;
* false conflation;
* synonym;
* cross-tradition alignment;
* reject.

Consumers therefore help Pāṭala discover its own missing vocabulary.

# 3. More interestingly: consumers generate **research questions**

There's a distinction between:

```text

```

and:

```text
RESEARCH CRUX:
"If vimarśa is constitutive of consciousness,
what exactly distinguishes its unrestricted and

```

You want the system learning that distinction automatically.

Perhaps:

```text
QuestionScore(Q) =

frequency
× graph centrality
× unresolvedness
× follow-up depth
× argument dependence
× evidence uncertainty
× novelty
```

Then Pāṭala starts discovering:

## Frequently asked

Good explainer candidate.

## Frequently misunderstood

Pedagogical defect.

## Frequently challenged

Argument-review candidate.

## Rare but highly central

Potential research crux.

## Common with no graph representation

Ontology gap.

## Common with conflicting answers

Scholar adjudication target.

This becomes **research prioritization by revealed human curiosity**.

And there is an important broader precedent: participatory/citizen-science research distinguishes between systems merely collecting data *from* the public and more participatory systems in which communities actually help formulate the research questions themselves. ([Nature][5])

That's much closer to what Pāṭala can become.

# 7. Consumer behavior can discover the actual structure of a field

There is research showing that learning pathways can be mined from huge behavioral logs as graphs rather than assumed in advance. One study extracted recurring learning “highways” from more than 800 million interactions involving over 3 million anonymized learners. ([arXiv][6])

Now apply that to philosophy.

We think the tradition should be learned:

```text
A → B → C → D
```

But observed humans repeatedly traverse:

```text
A → F → H → C
```

Why?

Maybe F/H constitute an unrecognized conceptual bridge.

That isn't just a curriculum insight.

It may reveal something about the **conceptual topology itself**.

For instance, perhaps everyone trying to understand `vimarśa` independently needs to understand:

> self-reference

even though our Sanskrit-centric ontology never created `self-reference` as a bridge concept.

Consumer behavior discovers that missing bridge.

# 14. You can deliberately insert tiny research tasks into ordinary consumption

This is perhaps the most practical design trick.

Don't ask:

> Help us annotate our graph.

Instead, while someone is learning:

> Which passage better supports this claim?

or:

> Do these two statements appear to disagree?

or:

> Which explanation answers the question more clearly?

or:

> Is this analogy misleading?

Their answer helps them learn **and simultaneously creates useful labels**.

That's the perfect externality:

```text
PEDAGOGICAL ACTIVITY
        =
DATA ACQUISITION ACTIVITY
```

Examples:

### Learner thinks they're doing:

argument challenge.

### Pāṭala gains:

argument validity annotation.

---

### Learner thinks they're doing:

compare two translations.

### Pāṭala gains:

preference pair / divergence label.

---

### Learner thinks they're doing:

spot the misconception.

### Pāṭala gains:

misconception benchmark.

---

### Learner thinks they're doing:

source hunt.

### Pāṭala gains:

EvidenceUse labels.

This is **learning as graph annotation**.

Very powerful.

But contribution labels should remain separate from authoritative scholarship: many-user agreement is evidence about interpretation/comprehension, not a substitute for expert adjudication.

Authenticated expertise and scoped adjudication.

Not everyone has the same vote.

Work on collective intelligence emphasizes that distributed groups can access a broader “perceptual field” of possible problems and solutions than isolated agents, although the design of aggregation and coordination is crucial. ([Nature][9])

That's essentially what we're building.

# 18. Every gap gets an Impact score

Something like:

[
I(g)=D\times C\times U\times E\times R
]

where:

* (D) = user demand;
* (C) = graph centrality;
* (U) = uncertainty;
* (E) = number of downstream explanations/lessons affected;
* (R) = research tractability.

Then:

```text
Gap queue
   ↓
research agent
   ↓
evidence gathered
   ↓
candidate resolution
   ↓
expert review if needed
   ↓
graph update
   ↓
dependency propagation
```

Consumer demand directly powers the **research scheduler**.

## The deepest framing

I would now distinguish **three engines** inside Pāṭala:

### The Truth Engine

> What can we justifiably say?

Sources → claims → arguments → review.

### The Understanding Engine

> What does this human understand, and what moves them forward?

Learner states → interventions → trajectories.

### The Discovery Engine

> What is the population revealing that we need to investigate next?

Questions → gaps → priorities → research → graph expansion.

And they recurse:

```text
TRUTH
  ↓
UNDERSTANDING
  ↓
DISCOVERY
  ↓
TRUTH'
  ↓
UNDERSTANDING'
  ↓
DISCOVERY'
```

**That third engine is the consumer moat.**

The consumer app isn't ultimately the simplified front end to Pāṭala.

It is the giant distributed sensing network through which Pāṭala discovers where its knowledge, ontology, arguments, pedagogy and scholarship need to grow next.

And that turns growth in users into growth in the **research object itself**, rather than merely growth in revenue or traffic.
