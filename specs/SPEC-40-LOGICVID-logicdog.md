# Logicvid — Does *Vimarśa* Explain Self-Organization?

## 1. What Levin has actually established

Levin’s empirical claim is stronger than “complexity emerges.”

Cell collectives do not merely execute a fixed bottom-up programme. They can respond to perturbations with novel compensatory actions while still approaching a larger anatomical target. Bioelectric networks formed by ion channels and gap junctions coordinate cellular behaviour, regulate gene expression, and help control organ-level morphology. Altering the bioelectric state can redirect growth without directly specifying every lower-level molecular action. ([Forms of life, forms of mind][1])

The causal sequence is approximately:

[
\text{bioelectric pattern}
\rightarrow
\text{distributed cellular coordination}
\rightarrow
\text{gene-expression changes}
\rightarrow
\text{altered anatomy}.
]

This is genuine top-down causation in the scientifically respectable sense:

[
\boxed{
\text{a higher-level system state constrains the trajectories of its components}.
}
]

It does not violate microphysics. The global pattern is implemented by local cells, but no isolated cell contains the complete anatomical goal. The target exists at the collective scale.

Levin’s current framework expands this into five principles: multiscale autonomy, self-assembly from active parts, continual reconstruction, exploitation of embodiment, and pervasive signalling that enables both self-organization and goal-directed top-down control. ([arXiv][2])

So far:

[
\boxed{
\text{local competencies can become a larger operative self}.
}
]

That is a major result.

## 2. The more speculative Levin move

Levin sometimes proposes reversing the ordinary ontology.

Instead of:

[
\text{body}=\text{agent},
\qquad
\text{bioelectric pattern}=\text{information used by the body},
]

he asks whether:

[
\text{bioelectric pattern}=\text{agent},
\qquad
\text{body}=\text{its slower memory medium}.
]

On this model, bioelectric patterns “read” and “write” into gene expression, chromatin, anatomy and biochemical state. Levin presents this as a speculative but experimentally actionable inversion, not an established result. ([Forms of life, forms of mind][3])

His 2026 “Platonic Space” proposal goes further: variable-agency patterns may not be wholly produced by physical matter but may be patterns that physical systems instantiate or access. Levin explicitly presents this as a research programme motivated by what he regards as the insufficiency of standard physicalism, not as an experimentally demonstrated ontology. ([Forms of life, forms of mind][4])

This matters because Levin himself is now approaching your question:

> Is organization generated entirely by physical interactions, or do physical systems instantiate more abstract agential patterns?

But even his stronger proposal does not establish phenomenal consciousness.

It establishes increasingly abstract **agency-patterns**.

# 3. What *vimarśa* does—and does not—mean

Your worry is correct:

> If *vimarśa* simply means “whatever appears,” it explains nothing.

But that is not the technical claim.

The Śaiva distinction is:

[
\textit{prakāśa}
================

\text{manifest actuality}
]

and:

[
\textit{vimarśa}
================

\text{manifest actuality’s capacity to determine, relate and enact itself}.
]

Bare *prakāśa* would be merely luminous presence.

*Vimarśa* introduces:

* self-reference;
* determination;
* differentiation;
* retention;
* recognition;
* willing;
* acting;
* contraction and expansion.

Dyczkowski’s reconstruction of Spanda is directly relevant here. Absolute consciousness is not a static witness but an active force whose recurring expansion and contraction generates novel perception and continuously renews the experienced world. The senses, thoughts and actions are subsidiary cycles of this universal conscious activity. ([JSTOR][5])

So Abhinavagupta is not saying:

[
\text{organization appears; therefore organization is consciousness}.
]

He is saying:

[
\boxed{
\text{the power by which a unity internally differentiates itself while remaining one is consciousness’s defining activity}.
}
]

That is more specific.

The structure is:

[
U
\rightarrow
{d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n}
]

while:

[
U
]

remains capable of integrating, relating and withdrawing those distinctions.

For Levin:

[
\text{cells}
\rightarrow
\text{collective bioelectric pattern}
\rightarrow
\text{organism-level goal}.
]

For Abhinavagupta:

[
\text{undivided consciousness}
\rightarrow
\text{differentiated powers and subjects}
\rightarrow
\text{integrated world}.
]

The structural correspondence is real:

[
\boxed{
\text{many differentiated processes become one coordinated agency}.
}
]

But the ontologies remain different.

# 4. Does *vimarśa* explain why organization occurs?

Not mechanistically.

Abhinavagupta does not give:

* equations for morphogenesis;
* mechanisms of cell communication;
* evolutionary pathways;
* gene-regulatory networks;
* a biological account of boundary formation.

Levin explains how specific forms of organization are physically implemented.

Abhinavagupta instead asks a prior metaphysical question:

> What must reality be like for self-organization, unified agency and determinate manifestation to be possible at all?

His answer is:

[
\boxed{
\text{reality cannot be fundamentally inert and externally related}.
}
]

Why?

Because an entirely inert relational structure provides:

[
\text{differences}
+
\text{causal transitions},
]

but seemingly not:

[
\text{a unity for which those differences form one meaningful world}.
]

Abhinavagupta identifies the latter with *vimarśa*.

Therefore, *vimarśa* is not offered as an efficient causal mechanism explaining how cells communicate. It is offered as the alleged **condition of possibility** of any process becoming:

* one process;
* self-related;
* goal-directed;
* differentiated from an environment;
* capable of treating states as its states.

This is analogous to saying:

[
\text{gravity explains why planets orbit}
]

while:

[
\text{spacetime geometry explains what gravitational organization consists in}.
]

Except Abhinavagupta’s claim is far more contestable.

# 6. Abhinavagupta’s strongest reply

He can accept every empirical mechanism.

He can say:

The Śaiva question is not:

> How is the pattern implemented?

It is:

> Why is the pattern intrinsically unified as a self-maintaining agency rather than merely describable as one by us?

Consider two descriptions:

### External description

[
X=
\text{a network of voltage states and molecular transitions}.
]

### Agential description

[
X=
\text{a system attempting to restore its target morphology}.
]

The second contains:

* identity;
* error;
* target;
* memory;
* success;
* failure;
* ownership of a future state.

Levin argues that the agential description earns its place because it improves prediction and intervention; morphogenesis is not adequately characterized as open-loop complexity alone. ([Forms of life, forms of mind][1])

Abhinavagupta pushes further:

> Where does the “for-itself” unity of the target come from?

If the target is real only in the scientist’s interpretation, then the organism does not genuinely possess goals.

If the target is objectively real for the organism, then reality contains intrinsic perspectival and normative structure.

So the bind is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Either agency is projected by an observer,}
}
]

or:

[
\boxed{
\text{agency is intrinsic to organized reality.}
}
]

Abhinavagupta chooses the second and names its most fundamental form *vimarśa*.

This is no longer empty—but it is still not a full proof.

# 7. The Spanda refinement

Dyczkowski helps sharpen what the active principle is supposed to be.

*Spanda* is not literal physical vibration.

It is the irreducible dynamism by which consciousness moves between:

[
\text{expanded, undivided subjectivity}
]

and:

[
\text{contracted, differentiated experience}.
]

The movement is not a journey through physical space. It is a variation in the scope and intensity of self-apprehension.

Dyczkowski describes perception as generated through the pulse of awareness between an expanded, undivided condition and a contracted individual condition. Novel perceptions arise because this contraction and expansion continually renews the experiential field. ([studylib.net][6])

Formally:

[
\text{expansion}
================

\text{many determinations apprehended as self}
]

[
\text{contraction}
==================

\text{only a restricted set apprehended as self}.
]

The key variable is not the amount of physical substance.

It is:

[
\boxed{
\text{the boundary of self-identification}.
}
]

This maps strikingly onto Levin’s cognitive light cone:

[
\text{self}
===========

\text{the scale of goals and states a system can sense and regulate}.
]

For a cell, the relevant “self” may be local metabolic viability.

For a tissue, it may be organ-level morphology.

For an animal, it includes distant possibilities, memories, social relations and long-term plans.

Levin explicitly defines selves partly through the scale of the goals they can pursue and recognizes first-person selfhood in terms of valence, attention and decision-making. ([Forms of life, forms of mind][1])

The correspondence is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Śaiva contraction}
\approx
\text{restriction of the cognitive and agential horizon}.
}
]

This is not identity. But it gives *saṃkoca* a much more precise modern correlate.

# 8. Does Śiva exist before contraction?

Yes—but **not temporally before**.

That distinction is essential.

Abhinavagupta is not proposing:

```text
First: Śiva existed alone.

Then: Śiva decided to contract.

Then: cells and animals appeared.
```

That would place Śiva inside time before the world, while time itself is supposed to arise within manifestation.

“Before” means:

[
\boxed{
\text{logically and ontologically prior}.
}
]

The uncontracted Śiva is not an earlier object.

It is the unrestricted form of the very power now functioning in contracted form.

Dyczkowski’s formulation is useful:

* the inner face is pure subjectivity beyond determinate spatial and temporal content;
* the outer face is that same consciousness becoming finite expression;
* Being and becoming are two poles of one continually self-renewing activity. ([Science and Nonduality (SAND)][7])

So:

[
\text{Śiva before contraction}
]

means:

[
\boxed{
\text{manifest agency before any particular boundary is treated as its exhaustive identity}.
}
]

It does **not** look like anything.

Anything that “looks like” something is already a determination:

[
\text{form}
\Rightarrow
\text{differentiation}
\Rightarrow
\text{some contraction}.
]

Uncontracted Śiva is therefore not:

* white light;
* an infinite visual field;
* cosmic space;
* a giant mind observing the universe.

It is the absence of any fixed limit on:

[
\text{what counts as self},
\qquad
\text{what can be known},
\qquad
\text{what can be enacted}.
]

Śiva is the unity of:

[
\text{manifestness}
+
\text{self-apprehension}
+
\text{freedom of determination}.

[
\boxed{
\text{Śiva}
===========

\textit{prakāśa}
+
\textit{vimarśa}

\textit{svātantrya}.

More precisely, these are not three separable parts.

They are three analytical descriptions of one structure:

* *prakāśa*: it is manifest;
* *vimarśa*: it is manifest to and as itself;
* *svātantrya*: its determinations arise from its own power rather than from something wholly external.

Spanda names the activity of this unity.

That is why a completely static pure witness would not count as Śiva. Dyczkowski stresses that Spanda theology rejects the merely witnessing Self in favour of an active, creative absolute. ([JSTOR][5])

It reveals that contraction cannot be total.

If contraction entirely eliminated Śiva’s nature, recognition would be impossible.

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{contraction must restrict expression without destroying the underlying capacity}.
}
]

Let unrestricted agency be:

[
A_\infty.
]

A finite organism expresses:

[
A_i\subset A_\infty.
]

But the finite form must retain enough of the underlying structure to:

* manifest a world;
* distinguish self and environment;
* act;
* remember;
* revise self-identification;
* recognize the restriction as restriction.

Thus:

[
\boxed{
\text{the finite subject is not other than Śiva;}
}
]

it is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Śiva operating under a locally binding self-model}.
}
]

Recognition is possible because the model constrains the range of identity without creating the manifest power that sustains the model.

In modern language:

[
\text{organismic self-model}
\neq
\text{the whole process making self-modelling possible}.
]

Recognition would be the realization that:

[
\boxed{
\text{I am not merely the bounded model through which this process currently regulates itself}.
}
]

# 12. Can a dog recognize itself as Śiva?

Here the answer splits.

## Ontological recognition

At the most minimal level, every sentient act already expresses nonconceptual self-apprehension:

[
\text{the dog’s perception is not alien to the dog’s experiencing}.
]

It sees, desires, remembers and acts from a point of view.

In that minimal sense, the dog already enacts:

[
\textit{aham-vimarśa}
]

a contracted first-person orientation.

So:

[
\boxed{
\text{the dog manifests Śiva’s recognitive structure}.
}
]

## Explicit liberating recognition

Can it realize:

[
\text{“I am the unrestricted consciousness manifesting all forms”}?
]

The available Śaiva philosophical sources do not give a clear, well-developed account of nonhuman animals attaining Pratyabhijñā liberation in their present animal embodiment. The tradition generally treats embodied beings as contracted forms of Śiva, but its philosophical arguments and prescribed means of recognition address cognitively and linguistically sophisticated human practitioners. I found no reliable primary passage establishing that a dog can perform the complete liberating recognition as a dog.

So the rigorous answer is:

[
\boxed{
\text{a dog is Śiva ontologically;}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{a dog displays minimal self-apprehension phenomenologically;}
}
]

but:

[
\boxed{
\text{whether a dog can attain full reflexive recognition is textually unresolved here}.
}
]

This exposes a serious pressure point.

If recognition requires Sanskrit instruction, conceptual discrimination or sophisticated metacognition, then liberation appears dependent on a specifically human cognitive architecture.

But if Śiva’s self-recognition is truly intrinsic, why should it require human symbolic cognition?

# 13. The dog objection cuts deeply

The dog forces Abhinavagupta to distinguish:

[
\text{being Śiva}
]

from:

[
\text{knowing oneself as Śiva}.
]

Suppose the dog is Śiva but cannot recognize this.

Then recognition depends on additional capacities:

[
R=
\text{manifestation}
+
\text{self-apprehension}
+
\text{metacognition}
+
\text{conceptual or nonconceptual decontraction}.
]

Now *vimarśa* itself divides:

### Minimal *vimarśa*

The capacity of a process to form a point of view and regulate a world.

### Reflective *vimarśa*

The capacity to model itself as the subject of that world.

### Liberating *vimarśa*

The capacity to dissolve the identification of subjectivity with one bounded model.

This is much more precise than saying everything is consciousness.

The developmental chain becomes:

[
\text{self-organization}
\rightarrow
\text{minimal perspective}
\rightarrow
\text{self-model}
\rightarrow
\text{model-of-the-self-model}
\rightarrow
\text{recognition of model as contraction}.
]

Levin helps explain the first two transitions.

Predictive cognition helps explain the third.

Metacognition may explain the fourth.

Abhinavagupta claims the fifth can reveal the unrestricted ground.

# 15. The deepest remaining objection

Abhinavagupta has not explained **why organization occurs in these particular forms**.

Saying:

> “It is Śiva’s freedom”

does not predict:

* why lipid membranes form;
* why voltage gradients organize morphology;
* why nervous systems evolved;
* why a dog has its particular sensory world;
* why one contraction produces a cell and another a human;
* why there is suffering rather than some other articulation.

At the mechanistic level:

[
\boxed{
\textit{svātantrya}
\text{ is not an alternative to biology}.
}
]

At the metaphysical level, however, biology still has not explained why organized regulation constitutes:

[
\text{a self}
]

rather than merely:

[
\text{a pattern we describe as a self}.
]

So the bind has become exact:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Levin explains how bounded agency is implemented but not why it is inwardly a self.}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Abhinavagupta declares bounded agency to be contracted selfhood but does not explain its specific implementation.}
}
]

Each has what the other lacks.

# The next Logicvid

The dog and cell cases force the next question:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{What is the minimum form of }vimarśa\textbf{?}
}
]

Is it:

1. any self-maintaining boundary;
2. goal-directed error correction;
3. valenced perspective;
4. phenomenal manifestation;
5. explicit self-awareness;
6. the capacity to recognize one’s own contraction?

Until these are separated, “everything is consciousness” remains too cheap.

But once separated, Abhinavagupta’s proposal becomes experimentally and philosophically sharper:

> Perhaps consciousness is not added when a sufficiently complex organism appears. Perhaps biological evolution progressively expands the range over which an already perspectival, self-articulating process can recognize and control itself.

The decisive test is then:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Can minimal agency exist without any inwardness whatsoever?}
}
]

If yes, Levin’s organization precedes *vimarśa*, and Abhinavagupta overreaches.

If no, then even the simplest genuine agent contains a contracted form of the structure Abhinavagupta calls consciousness.
