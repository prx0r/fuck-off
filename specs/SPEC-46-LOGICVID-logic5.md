Yes. This is much closer to the standard you want. After looking through the literature, I think the first thing to do is **not answer the question**. The first thing is to show that four different research communities are using the same words differently. That's the actual frontier.

# Logicvid 05 — What Is Presence?

## Point of Contention

After the previous Logicvid we concluded:

[
\boxed{
\text{Reality is manifest.}
}
]

Abhinavagupta calls this **prakāśa**.

But a problem immediately appears.

Nearly every consciousness theory quietly treats the following words as interchangeable:

* Presence
* Manifestation
* Consciousness
* Experience

Are they?

Or are they four different phenomena?

If they are different, then much of the consciousness debate is simply a confusion of definitions.

---

# Abhinavagupta's starting point

Abhinavagupta never begins with "consciousness."

He begins with **prakāśa**.

Why?

Because "consciousness" already suggests:

* a subject,
* a mind,
* an experiencer.

Prakāśa suggests none of these.

It means only:

> **that by virtue of which anything appears at all.**

Only afterwards does he introduce:

[
\textit{vimarśa}
]

the self-articulation of manifestation,

and then

[
\textit{spanda}
]

the dynamic unfolding of that articulation. Scholarly treatments consistently describe Abhinavagupta's consciousness as bimodal: *prakāśa* ("shining forth" or manifestation) together with *vimarśa* (self-reflexive active awareness), rather than mere passive illumination. ([mdpi.com][1])

---

# First distinction

## Manifestation

Manifestation answers one question:

> **Why is there appearing rather than non-appearing?**

It is the event of disclosure.

---

## Presence

Presence answers a different question:

> **What is the mode of that disclosure?**

This distinction already exists in contemporary phenomenology.

Phenomenologists define consciousness not as an inner object but as **presence-of**—the coming-to-appearance of whatever is experienced. Presence is not another thing inside experience; it is the very occurring of appearance. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov][2])

Therefore

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Manifestation}
\neq
\textbf{Presence}
}
]

Manifestation is

> appearing.

Presence is

> the actuality of what appears.

---

# Is Presence Consciousness?

Now the ambiguity begins.

There are at least three different meanings of "consciousness."

## Meaning 1

Consciousness = presence.

This is closest to phenomenology.

Consciousness simply is

the occurring of appearance.

---

## Meaning 2

Consciousness = self-aware presence.

This is Abhinavagupta.

Presence alone is insufficient.

Presence must also possess

[
\textit{vimarśa}
]

self-apprehension.

---

## Meaning 3

Consciousness = a conscious subject.

This is common in analytic philosophy and neuroscience.

Here consciousness already presupposes

someone

who has experiences.

These are radically different definitions.

Most debates never separate them.

---

# Is Presence Experience?

This distinction is much cleaner.

Every experience has content.

For example:

* red
* pain
* sound
* thought
* memory
* body
* time

Experience therefore has structure.

[
E
=

P
+
S
]

where

[
S
=

\text{phenomenal structure}.
]

Presence itself has no such commitment.

Presence says only

> something is there.

Experience says

> something appears **as** something.

Thus

[
\boxed{
Experience
==========

Presence
+
Determination.
}
]

Abhinavagupta would say

*vimarśa*

is precisely what gives manifestation this determinate structure.

---

# Is Presence Awareness?

This is even more subtle.

Awareness appears relational.

It suggests

[
x
\text{ is aware of }
y.
]

Presence need not.

Presence only requires

appearance.

No subject-object relation has yet been introduced.

This is why some phenomenologists argue that self-presence is **not** another act directed at consciousness. Consciousness is present simply by being the presence of whatever appears; treating it as a second object leads to regress. ([link.springer.com][3])

Therefore

[
\boxed{
Presence
\text{ is logically prior to awareness.}
}
]

---

# The theorem

The words are not equivalent.

### Prakāśa

Reality is manifest.

---

### Presence

The actuality of manifestation.

---

### Experience

Structured presence.

---

### Consciousness

An ambiguous term that may mean:

* presence,
* self-present presence,
* or the subject to whom presence belongs.

Those are three different ontologies.

---

# What has actually been established?

Abhinavagupta has established less than people usually think.

He has **not yet** proved:

* one Self,
* Śiva,
* universal consciousness.

He has established something much smaller:

[
\boxed{
Reality is manifest before it is interpreted as belonging to a subject.
}
]

Everything beyond that requires additional argument.

---

# The frontier

One primitive now remains.

If presence is more fundamental than experience,

and more primitive than a subject,

then

[
\boxed{
\textbf{What turns mere presence into conscious presence?}
}
]

Or, in Abhinavagupta's language:

> Why is *prakāśa* always accompanied by *vimarśa*?

That is the next genuine pressure point. It is also the exact place where Abhinavagupta, Buddhist epistemologists, phenomenology, and contemporary consciousness science begin to diverge.
